## Nobody reads the listing

They scan it. We have watched merchants pick apps over a shared screen, and the pattern barely varies: the tagline, the first screenshot, the review count, the price. If those four agree with each other, they install. If any one of them contradicts the others, they go back to search results.

That is roughly eight seconds of attention for something you spent nine months building.

## The four things they check

- **The tagline, read as a promise.** "Bulk edit products" is a promise. "The most powerful product management suite for modern commerce" is noise, and a merchant reads noise as a lack of confidence.
- **The first screenshot, read as proof.** Not a hero graphic with your logo on it — the actual interface, doing the actual thing, with realistic data in it.
- **The review count, read as risk.** Below about twenty reviews, merchants discount the rating entirely and start looking for a reason to trust you instead.
- **The price, read as a category.** Under $10 reads as a utility. Around $30 reads as a tool. Over $100 reads as something that needs a meeting.

## What makes them bounce

The clearest signal we have found is contradiction. A polished listing with a two-week-old changelog. A five-star rating on eleven reviews with a one-star review on top explaining that support never replied. A free plan whose limits are not on the pricing table.

## What we changed

We rewrote our own taglines to name the job rather than the category, replaced every marketing graphic with a real screenshot, and started answering reviews the same week they land. Install rate moved. More usefully, the merchants who installed were the ones the app was built for, which is the number that actually pays.

None of that is growth hacking. It is closing the gap between what the listing says and what the app is.
