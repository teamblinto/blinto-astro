## The $9 mistake

Nine dollars was not a price. It was a way of avoiding the conversation. We had no idea what the app was worth to a merchant doing $40,000 a month, so we picked a number low enough that nobody would argue with it — and then spent six months wondering why the merchants who installed it churned in three weeks.

The problem with a safe price is that it recruits the wrong customers. At $9 we attracted stores testing an idea, not stores running a business. They installed on a Friday, poked at it, and uninstalled before the trial ended. Support volume went up. Revenue did not.

## What merchants actually measure

Every merchant we interviewed had a number in their head before they opened the App Store. It was never "how much does this app cost". It was one of these:

- Hours a week this saves the person who currently does it by hand.
- Orders a month it recovers that would otherwise have been lost.
- The cost of the tool it replaces, usually a spreadsheet and a VA.
- Whether it will still be maintained in a year — the quiet one nobody says out loud.

## Eighteen experiments, three that mattered

We ran everything: annual discounts, a free tier, usage bands, a one-off lifetime deal we still regret. Three changes moved retention. The rest moved a chart for a fortnight and then moved back.

The first was raising the price. Going from $9 to $29 cut installs by about a third and roughly tripled the share of those installs still paying at day 90. The merchants who balked at $29 were the ones who were never going to keep it.

The second was removing the free tier. A free tier sounds like a funnel and behaves like a support contract: it drew in stores with no intention of upgrading and gave them a channel to ask us for features. A 14-day trial with the full feature set converts better and costs less to run.

The third was writing the price on the listing in the merchant's units. "From $29/month" is a number. "$29/month — about the cost of one recovered order" is an argument.

> The price that worked was the one a merchant could justify to themselves in a single sentence.

## Where we landed

$29 a month, one plan, no usage tiers. Higher than we were comfortable with, lower than the value it replaces, and simple enough that nobody needs a calculator to decide. Trial conversion went up. Support volume went down, because the merchants who install now have a reason to.

Tiering is the obvious next question, and we keep deciding against it. Every tier is a decision we hand to the merchant at the exact moment they are trying to decide something else. One plan means the only question on the page is whether the app is worth it.

## What we would do differently

We would talk to twenty merchants before writing the pricing page, not after. Every number we eventually landed on was already in those conversations — we just had not had them yet.

And we would treat the price as part of the product rather than as a setting. Changing it meant changing the listing copy, the onboarding, the trial length and what we said no to. That is a product decision. It deserved more than a guess.
